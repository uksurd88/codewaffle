## LinkedIn — Day 3: The Specifics

GPCRs were supposed to be the hard part of antibody discovery.

They sit in the membrane, refuse to crystallize politely, and most campaigns die at antigen production. A recent Nature Communications paper reports actual nanobody binders to a GPCR, discovered in silico with AlphaFold-Multimer. No antigen. Sequence and structure, straight to validated hits.

Caveats are real. AlphaFold-Multimer still hallucinates interfaces, and a binder is not a drug. But the cost curve is the story. If you can shortlist nanobody candidates from sequence alone for a GPCR, you can do it for almost anything else on the membrane.

That changes which targets are worth a program.

## LinkedIn — Day 5: The Question

A Rep-Seq run gives you thousands of paired heavy/light sequences. You have capacity to express maybe a few hundred.

How are you actually picking?

Most teams I talk to are still ranking on frequency, basic clonotype clustering, and gut feel. BCR models are finally getting good enough to do better, but "better" needs to mean predicting which clonotypes are worth the wet-lab spend, not topping another embedding benchmark.

What does your shortlist process look like, and where does it break?

## LinkedIn — Day 7: The Personal Take

What I keep coming back to this week is what our customers actually pay for.

As a Project Lead working on repertoire platforms, it is tempting to think the value is the data: paired heavy/light sequences, clonotype tables, neat exports. It is not. Storage is a commodity. The value is ranking that data well enough that someone is willing to put a candidate from your shortlist into a patient.

Alethio's mutation-agnostic bet on essential thrombocythemia only makes sense if upstream discovery is cheap enough to fail a few times. That is the bar now. Platforms either help teams be braver, or they get bypassed.
