GPCRs in silico, netrin1 in the clinic, and a bat receptor nobody was looking for. This week reads like a snapshot of where antibody discovery actually is, not where the marketing deck says it is.

## Nanobodies against GPCRs, without the library

Harvey, Smith and Hurley (Nat Commun, Apr 23) report in silico discovery of nanobody binders to a G-protein coupled receptor using AlphaFold-Multimer. No immunization, no phage panning as the entry point. Screen sequences, predict complexes, rank, test.

GPCRs are the hard case. They are membrane proteins, conformationally slippery, and the reason most antibody programs against them stall before hit validation. If AF-Multimer can enrich for real binders here, the same loop works for easier targets with less drama.

The honest caveat: "discovery" in silico still means a short list you have to express and measure. The win is not skipping the wet lab. It is starting with ten candidates instead of ten thousand. That changes what a discovery team looks like more than it changes the science.

## Netrin1, pancreatic cancer, and the unfashionable target

The Lap-NET1 phase 1b trial (Nature, Apr 22) combines NP137, an anti-netrin1 antibody, with modified FOLFIRINOX in locally advanced pancreatic cancer. Well tolerated, improved PFS and OS, mechanism tied to blocking EMT.

Netrin1 is not a target most pipelines would have prioritised five years ago. It is developmental biology, axon guidance, not the usual checkpoint or tumor antigen. The paper is a reminder that antibody MoA space is still wide open once you stop re-drugging the same twenty receptors.

Pancreatic is also the right place to run this kind of bet. The bar for "meaningful" is low enough that a real signal is visible in a phase 1b, and the standard of care is ugly enough that adding one more infusion is not the blocker.

## A bat receptor we did not ask for

Two bat papers landed together. Cardioderma cor alphacoronaviruses use human CEACAM6 to enter cells (Nature, Apr 22). And bat GBP5 shows lineage-specific positive selection shaping virus-specific restriction (PLOS Biology, Apr 21).

CEACAM6 is widely expressed in human lung. That is the uncomfortable sentence. This is not a spike-ACE2 story where the receptor was obvious; it is a different surface protein, in a different bat, and the virus was ready for us before we were looking.

The GBP5 work is the mirror image: why bats tolerate what we would not. Together they are a target-discovery pipeline nobody funded on purpose. If I were running pandemic preparedness antibody programs, I would be pulling CEACAM6-blocking leads into the freezer this quarter, not after the next outbreak.

## What I take from this week

The three papers map onto the three real levers in antibody discovery right now: compute that narrows the search space, clinical courage on unfashionable targets, and surveillance biology that tells you where to point the machine next. Each one is weak alone. The interesting programs in 2027 will be the teams that run all three as one loop, not three departments.

The overhyped part is still "AI-designed antibodies" as a standalone story. The underhyped part is what happens when structure prediction, repertoire data, and unglamorous target biology sit in the same platform and the same weekly meeting.
